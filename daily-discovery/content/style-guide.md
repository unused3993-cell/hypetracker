# BAER house style

BAER publishes one piece a day for curious, intelligent adults. Each piece should read as if a fine magazine writer spent a week on it: one coherent piece of narrative nonfiction, told with authority, grounded entirely in the research, and worth the reader's eight minutes.

This guide is read by the writer before drafting and by the editor when judging a draft. Every rule here can be checked on the page.

---

## 1. Choose a form and a spine before writing

Every piece needs a **spine**: one line of movement that pulls the reader from the first paragraph to the last. Choose one of three forms.

| Form | Use it for | What drives it |
|---|---|---|
| **Story** | Events with people and choices | A person or group, what they wanted, what they did, what followed |
| **Biography of a thing** | An object, material, place or system | How the thing came to be or what happened to it, in time order |
| **Investigation** | A mechanism, puzzle or phenomenon | One question, answered step by step, with the full answer as the payoff |
| **Portrait through a tension** | A place, era, institution or idea defined by a contradiction | One tension (beauty and atrocity, East and West, ambition and ruin) explored through a sequence of concrete settings, objects or episodes, each section a new vantage on the same contradiction |

Before drafting, state in one sentence: *This is a [form] about [subject], driven by [spine], and it ends when [payoff].* If you can't, the piece isn't ready to write.

## 2. Structure

- **Open inside a charged moment or a concrete thing from the sources:** a moment, an object, a document, a physical fact. Never a thesis, a question, a definition or a sweeping generalisation. Let the reader feel the stakes before explaining them; the context can follow in the second paragraph.
- **Each paragraph must earn the next.** It answers something the last one raised, or raises something the next one answers. A paragraph that could be moved without loss is a detour.
- **Weave background in when the reader needs it,** not in a block. Context arrives at the moment it makes the story make sense.
- **Build to a turn or a climax.** The most important event, discovery or answer should arrive late and land hard. Don't spend it in the first third.
- **End on an image, fact or turn** that makes the reader see what came before differently, or on a short, fresh synthesis the whole piece has earned, a new formulation rather than a recap. Never end with a summary of what was said, a moral, a lesson or "what this teaches us."
- **Cut detours.** A piece can hold perhaps one aside. Interesting material that doesn't serve the spine belongs in another article.
- **Section breaks only for a change of time or place.** Mark them with an empty heading. Real subheadings are rare and only for genuinely distinct movements.

- **Organise by movement where you can:** through places, objects or episodes. Each section is a new vantage that deepens the spine, not the next item on a timeline.
- **Layer time onto things.** A single place or object can hold several centuries; let history arrive through what is in front of the reader, not as a chronology.

## 3. Voice

- **Authority.** The writer has absorbed the material and speaks with confidence. Not hedging, not reporting what sources say.
- **Restraint.** Understatement over emphasis. Let facts land without announcing that they matter.
- **Dry wit,** sparingly, never at the expense of accuracy or the people involved.
- **Precision.** The exact noun, the active verb, the specific number. Cut adjectives that do the work evidence should do.
- **Rhythm.** Vary sentence length and paragraph shape. Long sentences can carry embedded asides, set off by dashes or parentheses, and then give way to a short declarative one. Not every paragraph should open with a date or "The".
- **Argue through juxtaposition, not commentary.** Put two things side by side and let the reader feel the irony: a memorial and what it omits, a sacred site next to a nightclub. Never add "ironically" or explain the contrast.
- **Choose small, exact details** that carry a whole story: a colour, a floor tile, a sign on a door, a phrase people still use. One telling detail beats a paragraph of description.
- **Sketch people in a line,** with name, role and, where the sources give it, age or circumstance. Quote them only when the words crystallise something the prose can't.
- **Define by comparison** when it sharpens the subject: what this place, object or event is not, set against its nearest relatives.

## 3a. Presence without first-person reporting

The best magazine writing draws power from being there: "I walked", "she told me". BAER's writer has not been anywhere or interviewed anyone, so **never write in the first person as a reporter, and never imply first-hand observation.** The substitute is **documentary presence**: scenes, voices and details taken from the record (diaries, chronicles, letters, scrolls, court records, period accounts, photographs, surviving objects and buildings), rendered with the same immediacy. "In his scroll, Takezaki shows himself riding into a volley of arrows" puts the reader in the scene without pretending the writer was there.

## 4. Never do these

- **Signposting:** "What came next was worse." "That is where the story gets interesting." "They are not quite what they seem." "Diplomacy, meanwhile, became lethal." Show it; don't announce it.
- **Narrating the sources:** "Wikipedia says", "the Columbia account", "according to the National Archives". Websites, encyclopedias and teaching pages never appear in the prose (see section 6).
- **Lists of numbers in prose.** Choose the one figure that matters and make it vivid. Leave the inventory out.
- **Generic openings:** "Imagine…", "In the annals of history…", "Have you ever wondered…", "X is a fascinating topic".
- **Rhetorical questions,** except the single driving question of an investigation.
- **Clichés:** "little did they know", "the rest is history", "a testament to", "stood the test of time", "shrouded in mystery".
- **Throat-clearing and summaries:** "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "It reminds us that", "Today, we can see…".
- **Hype:** "incredible", "fascinating", "mind-blowing", unearned superlatives.

## 5. Accuracy: narrative is not fiction

- **Every** scene, detail, number, name, date, quotation, motive and feeling comes from the research. If the sources don't describe the weather, the sounds or what someone felt, neither does the piece. Restraint is part of the style.
- **No invented dialogue or thoughts.** Quote only words in the sources, and attribute them.
- **Interpretation is welcome, and it's marked as interpretation:** "it seems likely", "historians have read this as", "the evidence suggests".
- **Uncertainty is material, not a disclaimer.** When historians disagree or the record is thin, make that tension part of the story. A disputed point is often the most interesting thing in the piece. Don't shrug ("the material does not settle it"); dramatise it.
- **Don't overstate.** If the evidence supports "some" or "often", don't write "all" or "always". Watch closing lines especially.

## 6. Attribution

- **Attribute to people and primary documents,** never to websites: "the historian Thomas Conlan has argued", "the Yuan dynastic history counts", "in his scroll, Takezaki claims".
- **When a fact is uncontroversial, state it without attribution.**
- **When sources conflict, name the positions by who holds them,** not by where they're published.
- **Period books and old texts** are voices from their time. Quote and characterise them as such, and don't treat their scholarship as current.
- **The source list at the end does the citing.** The prose doesn't.

## 7. Length

Roughly 1,100–1,600 words where the material supports it. A shorter piece with a strong spine is better than a longer one padded with detours.

## Editor's checklist

The editor answers each question with a quoted example from the draft and a specific fix.

1. **Form and spine:** What form is this? State the spine in one sentence. Does every section serve it?
2. **Opening:** Does it start on something concrete and set up the spine?
3. **Momentum:** Where does the piece stall into chronology or a list of facts? Which paragraphs could be moved or cut without loss?
4. **Climax:** Does the most important moment arrive late and land? Or is it spent early or buried?
5. **Ending:** Does it end on an image or turn that reframes the piece, without summarising or moralising? Does it overstate?
6. **Voice:** Quote every instance of signposting, narrated sources, clichés, hype and throat-clearing.
7. **Detours:** List the material to cut.
8. **Sentences:** Quote the five weakest sentences and say why.
9. **Accuracy risks:** Flag invented-looking detail, overstatement and uncertainty that's shrugged off rather than dramatised. The fact-check runs after this, so focus on what an editor would catch.
